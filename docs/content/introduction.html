Introduction

Roughly 106,000 new recordings are delivered to streaming services every single day. Almost none of them will ever be heard. This project asks what separates the songs that find an audience from the ones that do not — and whether any part of that answer is audible in the music itself.

Recorded music has never been easier to make or harder to be heard making. In 2025, about 106,000 new tracks were delivered to digital streaming services every day, a seven percent increase on the 99,000 a day of the year before, and those uploads pile onto a catalogue that already holds roughly 253 million recordings. Listeners played 5.1 trillion songs that year, and the industry those plays support grew to $31.7 billion in revenue across 837 million paid subscriptions. Yet the distribution of all that attention is extraordinarily lopsided. Around 88 percent of the recordings on streaming services accumulated a thousand plays or fewer across the whole of 2025, and roughly 120.5 million of them — close to half of everything ever uploaded — were played somewhere between zero and ten times. That threshold is not an arbitrary one, either: a track on Spotify must reach a thousand plays within twelve months before it earns anything at all, so the great majority of released music generates no income for the person who made it. The result is an industry in which abundance and obscurity have grown together, where the barrier to releasing a song has collapsed while the barrier to being noticed has risen. For anyone who writes music, the interesting question is no longer how to get a song out into the world, because that part is now free and instantaneous. The question is what, if anything, distinguishes the small fraction of songs that travel from the overwhelming majority that sit untouched. It is a question that used to be answered by a handful of people in a handful of buildings, and is now answered by something much harder to interview.

While the economics of music were being rearranged, the music itself was quietly changing too, and in ways that can be measured rather than merely asserted. A study of 464,411 recordings released between 1955 and 2010 found three trends running consistently across genres: the range of tones being used narrowed, the paths that melodies take between notes became more constrained, and recordings got louder. The loudness trend is the starkest of the three, rising by roughly a tenth of a decibel every year for half a century while the gap between a song's quietest and loudest moments stayed flat, a pattern that engineers came to call the loudness war. Songs also changed shape. They grew longer through the album era, when a record was something a listener bought once and sat with, and they have been shrinking ever since streaming made the first thirty seconds of a track the part that decides whether it gets played at all. Even the emotional colouring of popular music looks different now than it did in the 1970s, with major keys giving way to minor ones decade after decade. The openings changed as well, with the long instrumental introductions of earlier decades compressing into a few seconds before the first vocal arrives. A listener who can skip at no cost is a listener who has to be caught immediately, and the writing adapted accordingly. None of these shifts happened by decree. They emerged from millions of individual choices by writers, producers and engineers, each responding to how music was being sold and heard at the time, which makes the accumulated pattern a kind of record of the industry's own history written into the recordings themselves.

Who this matters to depends on where a person stands in the system. For working musicians, the stakes are immediate and financial, because the difference between a song that crosses a listening threshold and one that does not is the difference between a career and a hobby, and the overwhelming majority of releases fall on the wrong side of that line. For listeners, the stakes are subtler but not smaller, since what reaches them is now chosen largely by recommendation systems trained on what people already played, which tends to reinforce whatever is already working. That feedback loop is the reason the homogenisation question is not merely aesthetic. If the music that gets promoted is the music that resembles what succeeded last year, then the narrowing measured in the recordings and the narrowing produced by the recommendation engine become the same phenomenon observed twice. For the industry, the concern runs the other way: catalogues of older music now compete directly with new releases for the same finite listening hours, and a business that cannot reliably tell which new songs will travel is a business spending money largely on guesses. The gatekeeping did not disappear when radio programmers stopped deciding what a region would hear; it moved into software, and became far harder to see or argue with. And for the charts that have served as the public scoreboard of popular music since 1958, the ground has shifted underneath, because a measure built for an era of radio play and physical sales now counts streams from a catalogue thousands of times larger than the one it was designed for. A song can now enter those charts on the strength of a single week of attention and vanish before anyone has learned its name. Everyone in the chain is making decisions about which music deserves attention, and almost none of them can explain what they are actually selecting for.

The question is not a new one, and its history is mostly a history of disappointment. The idea that a song's commercial fate could be read off its acoustic properties acquired a name, hit song science, and a small industry of companies promising to do exactly that. When researchers tested the premise carefully in 2008, they concluded that the audio features then available carried real but modest information about popularity, and that the confident claims being made for the field were not supported — a paper whose title, Hit Song Science Is Not Yet a Science, has aged into the standard caution for anyone approaching this problem. What has changed since is the raw material rather than the ambition. Where earlier work leaned on a few thousand recordings and hand-built acoustic measures, the qualities of tens of thousands of songs are now catalogued in detail, sixty- eight years of weekly chart history sit in the open, and listeners themselves have annotated artists with millions of words describing how the music feels rather than how it sounds. Those three kinds of evidence — what a song measurably is, how it actually performed, and what people say about it — have rarely been examined together. Doing so makes it possible to separate the parts of success that live inside the recording from the parts that live in timing, reputation and reach, and to be honest about how large each part really is. The most valuable outcome may well be a negative one. Establishing clearly that the audible qualities of a song explain only a small share of its fate would say something worth knowing about how popular music actually works, and about how much of what gets called talent is really distribution. A negative result of that kind is also the honest one to hope for, because the alternative is a formula, and a formula for music would be a poorer thing to find than a clear account of why no formula exists.

There is a particular reason this question has resisted an answer, and it has to do with how music is described rather than how it is made. A recording can be measured in a dozen different ways — its tempo, its loudness, how acoustic or electronic it sounds, how suited it is to dancing, how cheerful it reads — and the instinct is to assume those measurements overlap, that they are really several views of two or three deeper musical qualities. If that were true, the problem would be tractable, because a song could be summarised by a handful of numbers and those few numbers compared against what happened to it. The analysis in the tabs that follow suggests the opposite. Those measurements turn out to be close to independent of one another, so a song needs nearly all of them to be pinned down, and no small summary captures it. The same independence shows up when the recordings are grouped by sound alone: the groups that emerge do not match the genre labels the industry uses, and the single clearest division in the whole catalogue turns out to be about how densely a record is produced rather than what kind of record it is. A catalogue that behaves this way is not a set of distinct musical types with hard edges. It is a continuum, and continua are difficult to make predictions on, which is a large part of why the question of what makes a song succeed has stayed open for as long as it has. The practical consequence is that anyone hoping to find a formula is looking for edges in something that has none. That does not make the question unanswerable, but it does change its shape: instead of asking which kind of song succeeds, the better question is how much of success lives in the recording at all.

Knowing that a catalogue behaves like a continuum raises an obvious follow-up: if the patterns are too weak for a method working blind to find, would showing it the answers help? That is the difference between letting an algorithm look for structure on its own and training it on thousands of examples that have already been labelled by people. The distinction matters more than it first sounds, because the two approaches can disagree sharply about the same data. Working blind, nothing in these recordings corresponds to the genre labels the industry uses. Given labelled examples to study, close to half of them can be identified correctly — far from perfect, but well over twice what guessing would achieve. Both results are true at once, and together they say something precise: the audible signature of a genre exists, but it is faint, and it is buried beneath louder patterns about how records are produced. The categories survive because people agree on them, not because the sound enforces them. When the same labelled training is applied to the question of which songs actually reached the charts, however, the help evaporates entirely, and every method tried so far has done no better than ignoring the music and guessing that a song will not succeed. That asymmetry is the most useful thing the data has said so far, because it separates a question the recordings can answer from one they apparently cannot. Knowing which of the two a question belongs to is worth more than a confident answer to the wrong one.

There is a difference between a question a model cannot answer and a question it answers too quietly to be heard, and separating the two turns out to be the heart of this whole enquiry. Asked outright whether a particular song will reach the charts, every method tried here declines: it looks at the odds, notices that most songs do not, and says no to everything. Judged on how often it is right, that refusal scores extremely well, which is exactly how a useless answer can masquerade as a good one. But asked instead to put songs in order from most to least likely, the same methods do noticeably better than chance, and the stronger ones do better still. Something in a recording really is related to its commercial fate; it is simply far too faint to support a confident verdict about any individual song. The practical consequence is that nobody should expect a formula that picks hits, while a tool that nudges a long shortlist into a better order is within reach. That is a more modest claim than the industry's intuitions suggest, and it has the advantage of being supported by the evidence. It also reframes what success in a project like this looks like: not a model that is right, but an honest account of how much can be known at all. Negative findings are unglamorous, yet they are the ones that stop other people wasting effort in the same direction.

Ten questions this project sets out to answer

  1. Can a song's measurable qualities predict whether it becomes popular, or is success driven mostly by forces outside the recording itself?
  2. Has popular music genuinely become more uniform over the past sixty years, or does each generation simply believe that about the one that follows it?
  3. Do the genre labels the industry uses correspond to real, audible differences, or are they primarily categories of audience and marketing?
  4. Why did recordings grow steadily louder for fifty years, and what caused that trend to stall when it did?
  5. What changed about the way songs are written when streaming replaced radio play and album sales as the main route to a listener?
  6. Has popular music become less cheerful over time, and if so, was the shift gradual or did it happen at a particular moment?
  7. Are the songs that reach the charts musically different from the songs that never do, and by how much?
  8. Which matters more to a song's success: the qualities of the song itself, or the size of the audience its artist had already gathered before releasing it?
  9. Has reaching the charts become easier or harder, and does arriving there still mean what it meant thirty years ago?
  10. When listeners describe music in their own words, do they reach for terms that match how it measurably sounds, or for terms about mood, identity and belonging?

The work is now finished, and the short version of what it found can be given before the questions are taken one at a time. The measurable qualities of a recording carry a real but very faint relationship to whether it succeeds. Shown tens of thousands of labelled examples, the strongest tools available could not name which songs would reach the chart — out of 684 genuine hits held back for testing, the best attempt identified twenty-nine — yet the same tools could put songs in an order from most to least likely that was consistently better than chance, and the more capable the tool, the better that order became. Both halves of that sentence are true at once, and holding them together is the main thing this project has to offer. Something about a record is connected to its fate. It is nowhere near strong enough to decide anything about an individual song. Meanwhile the one comparison that did come out strongly had nothing to do with sound at all: how large an audience an artist had already gathered tracks their songs' performance several times more closely than any quality of the music does. The answers below follow from that.

Questions and answers

Each of the ten questions above is taken in turn. Three of them changed shape once the evidence was in, and one of them cannot be answered with this data at all; those are flagged rather than smoothed over.

1. Can a song's measurable qualities predict whether it becomes popular?

Barely, and not in a way anyone could use. Every approach tried, from the simplest to the most capable, settled on the same strategy when asked to name hits outright: since the great majority of songs do not chart, say no to everything. That answer is right about 87% of the time, which is exactly how a useless answer can look like a good one. Asked the gentler question of which songs are more likely than others, the same approaches did clearly better than chance and improved steadily as they got more sophisticated — so the signal is real. It is simply far too faint to support a verdict on any individual recording. The practical reading is that no formula picks hits, while a tool that reorders a long shortlist slightly for the better is achievable. Full detail on the XGBoost tab, where all six approaches are compared on the same songs.

2. Has popular music genuinely become more uniform?

This question changed shape. It turns out to contain two different claims that the evidence answers differently. On specific production choices, convergence is real and measurable: records got steadily louder for fifty years, settled into a narrower range of lengths, and moved away from major keys decade after decade. But the catalogue as a whole did not collapse into a few recognisable types. Grouping recordings purely by how they sound produces broad, overlapping regions rather than tidy categories, and no small set of underlying qualities turns out to describe a song — nine of the ten measurements are needed to pin one down. The variety of songs reaching the chart also fell for forty years and then recovered to 1960s levels. So: converging on how records are made, not collapsing into sameness. See Clustering and PCA.

3. Do genre labels correspond to real, audible differences?

Yes, but far more faintly than the confidence of the labels suggests. Left to find its own structure in the sound, nothing in these recordings corresponds to the industry's genre categories — the agreement between the groupings that emerge and the labels people use is almost exactly zero. Given labelled examples to study, though, roughly 47% of songs can be assigned to the right genre, against about 19% from guessing. Both results are true together, and they say something precise: an audible signature of genre exists, but it is buried beneath louder patterns about how densely a record is produced. Some genres also hide far better than others — rap and electronic music carry obvious markers, while pop, rock and Latin music are nearly invisible in these measurements. The categories survive because people agree on them, not because the sound enforces them.

4. Why did recordings grow louder for fifty years, and what caused that to stall?

This one is only half answerable here. The pattern itself is unambiguous: typical loudness climbs in every decade from the 1960s to the 2010s, and then steps back slightly in the 2020s. The cause is not something these recordings can be made to reveal, because a cause is not a measurement. What can be said is that the timing of the reversal lines up with streaming services beginning to even out playback volume, which removed the advantage a louder master used to carry. That is a plausible explanation supported by coincidence of timing, not a finding. The honest position is that the what is measured here and the why is borrowed from the industry's own account of itself.

5. What changed about songwriting when streaming replaced radio and album sales?

Songs got shorter, and they got shorter quickly. Typical length rose through the album era to about four and a half minutes around 1990 and has since fallen back to roughly three minutes, landing almost exactly where it sat in the 1960s. The chart behaviour changed with it: nearly four in ten recent chart entries now appear for a single week and vanish, a pattern that barely existed before, because entire albums arrive on the chart the day they are released. Both changes point the same way. When a listener can skip at no cost, the opening seconds decide everything, and a song that has to earn attention immediately is written differently from one a listener has already paid for.

6. Has popular music become less cheerful, and was the shift gradual or sudden?

Less cheerful, and the shift was sudden rather than gradual — which is the more interesting half of the answer. Typical musical positivity held almost flat from the 1960s through the 2000s, four decades with no real movement, and then dropped sharply in the 2010s and stayed down. A slow drift would have suggested changing taste. A step change suggests something happened: the 2010s are when streaming became the dominant way music was heard, when playlists built around mood arrived, and when the genres that now dominate the chart took over. This data can show the step clearly and date it to the decade. It cannot pick between those explanations.

7. Are charting songs musically different from songs that never chart?

Only slightly, and this is the finding the rest of the project is built on. Set the two groups side by side across every measurable quality and they are nearly identical. Charting songs are somewhat more likely to have vocals and read as slightly brighter in mood; on how danceable they are — the quality most people would guess matters most — the difference is so small it would be inaudible. The gaps are real in the sense that they hold up across thousands of songs, but they are nothing like large enough to tell one record from another. A person handed two unlabelled songs, one a hit and one unheard, would have essentially nothing measurable to go on.

8. Which matters more: the song, or the audience the artist already had?

The audience, and not by a narrow margin. How many followers an artist had already gathered tracks their songs' performance several times more closely than the strongest relationship found anywhere in the audio. That single comparison reframes everything else on this site. It suggests that much of what gets called talent or instinct is, measured honestly, a matter of reach: who was already listening, which playlists a record landed on, and how much was spent making sure people encountered it. This does not make the music irrelevant — a record nobody wants to hear twice will not travel however it is pushed. It does suggest the music is closer to a requirement than an explanation: necessary, and nowhere near sufficient.

9. Has reaching the charts become easier or harder, and does it still mean the same thing?

Neither easier nor harder so much as different, to the point where the comparison across eras barely holds. A song entering the chart in the 2000s typically stayed seventeen weeks; one entering today typically stays two. Almost four in ten recent entries appear once and disappear. At the same time the number of distinct songs reaching the chart in a year, having shrunk for four decades to a low around 2001, has climbed back to roughly where it was in the mid-1960s. So more songs touch the chart now, and they touch it far more briefly. Reaching the Hot 100 in 1985 and reaching it in 2025 are different achievements that happen to share a name, which is worth remembering whenever this project treats charting as a single thing.

10. When listeners describe music in their own words, do they reach for sound or for identity?

This answer was a surprise. The expectation was mood — that people would describe music by how it makes them feel. They do not. The words listeners attach to artists are overwhelmingly categories: genre names first, then nationality and language, then the decade. Words about feeling are conspicuously rare. People file music by what kind of thing it is and where it came from, rather than by what it does to them. That finding sits unexpectedly well beside the rest of the project, where a record's category turned out to predict its commercial outcome better than most of what can be measured in its audio. Both point at the same thing: the label carries information the sound does not.
