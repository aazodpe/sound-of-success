XGBoost

This is the last method the project will try, and it is the one the rest of the field would reach for first. If a heavily tuned XGBoost model cannot predict which songs chart, the limit is not in the machinery.

Overview

XGBoost is gradient boosting — the running sum of small trees described on the previous tab — with the engineering taken seriously. Two of its additions change the results rather than merely the speed, and both are answers to the problem that tab ended on: a boosted model will happily keep fitting until it has memorised the training set, and nothing in the training output will warn anyone.

The first addition is a regularised objective. Ordinary gradient boosting grows each tree to minimise the training loss and nothing else, so a tree that splits into many leaves with large, confident values always looks better than a cautious one. XGBoost changes the quantity being minimised, adding a price for complexity directly into it: objective = loss + γT + ½λ∑w². Here T is the number of leaves the tree has grown and w is the value sitting in each leaf. The γT term charges a flat fee per leaf, so a split has to buy more loss reduction than it costs or it is not made at all. The λ∑w² term charges by the square of each leaf's value, so one very confident leaf costs far more than several hesitant ones, and the whole tree is pulled toward modest corrections. Neither term is a filter applied afterwards; both are inside the thing the algorithm is minimising, which means the tree that gets built is already the one that balances fit against complexity.

The second addition is early stopping. On the previous tab the number of trees had to be recovered after the fact, by fitting 300 of them and replaying the sequence to find where the validation score peaked. XGBoost does that during training. A validation set is handed to the fitting routine, which scores it after every round and keeps count of how long it has been since the score improved. Once that count reaches a set patience — forty rounds here — training halts and the model from the best round is the one kept. The number of trees stops being a setting anyone has to guess and becomes something the data decides, differently for every configuration.

XGBoost also handles missing values natively, grows trees level by level with a histogram approximation that makes it fast on large data, and samples columns as well as rows. None of those matter much on a table of 25,817 complete rows and 30 columns, and they are mentioned here only so the reasons it is not faster or better on this particular problem are clear.

Data Prep

Identical partitions to AdaBoost and gradient boosting, deliberately, so that the comparison table at the end of this tab compares six methods on one dataset rather than six methods on six: 15,489 training songs, 5,164 validation and 5,164 test, verified disjoint, with the test set the same rows Module 3 used. The target is charted, which 684 of the test songs are.

The one-hot step, and why it is still needed. Three of the most useful columns in the cleaned data are text: a song's genre, its musical key and its mode. XGBoost works on numbers, so these have to be converted, and the obvious conversion is the wrong one. Numbering the genres pop = 1, rap = 2, rock = 3 would invent an ordering that does not exist, and a tree would then be free to split on genre ≤ 2 — a question that groups pop with rap for no reason other than alphabetical accident. One-hot encoding avoids this by giving every level a column of its own holding 1 or 0, so the only question a tree can ask is "is this song rap?", which is a real question. The cost is width: three columns become twenty, and the feature count goes from 10 to 30. For a tree-based method that cost is cheap, since a binary indicator is the easiest possible thing to split on. It is worth noting that modern XGBoost can accept categorical columns directly with enable_categorical; the explicit encoding is kept here because it is the same matrix the previous two tabs were given, and swapping it would make the comparison dishonest.

Code

Python 3 with the xgboost package, version 3.2.0, through its scikit-learn interface (XGBClassifier). Early stopping is configured on the estimator with early_stopping_rounds=40 and the validation partition passed as eval_set; the objective is binary:logistic and the metric watched is auc, for the reason every tab since Module 3 has given — accuracy cannot tell these models apart. The same script also assembles the comparison table below, and it does so carefully: accuracy and recall for the earlier models are read from the metrics files those tabs published, so the table cannot contradict the page it came from, while the two ranking scores that were never recorded are recomputed by refitting those exact models, with an assertion that each refit reproduces its published accuracy to twelve decimal places before its score is used.

Results

Four tree depths (2, 3, 5, 7) were crossed with three learning rates (0.03, 0.1, 0.3) and two values of the regularisation strength λ (1 and 10), giving 24 configurations. Each was capped at 800 rounds and allowed to stop early, so the number of trees varied by configuration from 10 rounds to 799 — a spread that no single fixed setting could have covered.

The regularisation term did something worth noticing, though not something large. At the chosen depth and rate, λ = 1 reached a validation AUC of 0.7666 and stopped after 35 rounds; λ = 10 reached 0.7673 and ran to 73. The heavier penalty did not make each tree better so much as it made each tree smaller, which left more worth doing and let the sequence run twice as long before it stopped improving. That is the regularisation working exactly as described in the Overview, and it bought 0.0006 AUC.

The one-hot columns helped for the third consecutive time. The chosen configuration on the ten numeric columns alone reaches a validation AUC of 0.723; with the twenty indicator columns added it reaches 0.767.

Which did best, and by how much? It depends entirely on which column is read, and that is the finding rather than a dodge.

On accuracy, AdaBoost wins at 86.81%, beating the do-nothing baseline by 0.06 percentage points — three songs out of 5,164. The gap between the best and worst of the six is 0.09 percentage points, or five songs. No sensible person would choose between these methods on that basis, and two of them actually score below the baseline.

On ranking, XGBoost wins at an ROC AUC of 0.766, ahead of gradient boosting by 0.0021 and ahead of Naive Bayes, the weakest of the six, by 0.112. That ordering is not noise: the six models line up by sophistication, and the three boosted ensembles separate cleanly from the three single models by about 0.08. Set the two columns beside each other and the contrast is stark — the ranking column spreads across 0.112 while the accuracy column spreads across 0.0010. The methods really do differ, and the difference lives somewhere accuracy cannot see.

And on the thing that would actually matter to someone trying to use any of this, all six fail together. The best model in the project found 29 of 684 hits. The worst found none. Six methods, four modules and a few hundred fitted models agree on the same answer.

Conclusions

XGBoost did what it was brought in to do, which was to establish a ceiling rather than to break one. It is the strongest result in the project by the measure that distinguishes these models — an ROC AUC of 0.766 on the held-out songs, ahead of everything else tried — reached with a regularised objective, a 24-configuration search and a stopping rule that watched the validation set round by round. The reward over ordinary gradient boosting, which searched a smaller grid, carried no explicit penalty on complexity and had to recover its tree count after the fact, was 0.0021 AUC. When the best available tool, tuned properly, beats a simpler version of itself by two parts in a thousand, the tool is no longer the constraint.

The comparison table is the most valuable thing this tab produces, and it is worth being precise about what it shows. Read down the accuracy column and the six methods are identical, all of them within a tenth of a percentage point of a strategy that involves no learning at all. Read down the ranking column and they are plainly not identical: they rise in a clean order from 0.655 to 0.766, the boosted ensembles pull away from the single models by a wide margin, and the ordering matches what anyone familiar with these methods would predict in advance. Both columns are honest measurements of the same six models on the same 5,164 songs. A reader given only the first would conclude that machine learning has nothing to offer this problem; a reader given only the second would conclude that it offers a great deal. The useful conclusion needs both: there is real, measurable signal connecting a recording to its commercial fate, the better methods extract more of it, and none of it is strong enough to support a confident statement about any individual song.

The one-hot experiment, run three times now with three different methods, has become the most consistent secondary finding in the project. Adding genre, key and mode lifted validation AUC from 0.701 to 0.745 for AdaBoost, from 0.713 to 0.762 for gradient boosting, and from 0.723 to 0.767 here. In XGBoost's accounting the genre indicators then took the overwhelming share of the finished model. The caution about comparing importance figures across implementations stands, but the direction does not depend on the measure: a record's category predicts its commercial outcome better than most of what can be measured in its audio. Module 2 found that those same categories are barely audible in the acoustic measurements — clustering by sound recovered genre at an adjusted Rand index of 0.020. Put the two findings together and the label is carrying information the sound does not, which is to say it is carrying information about how a record is marketed, promoted and placed rather than about how it was made.

What this tab cannot settle is whether the ceiling belongs to the data or to the world. The honest possibilities are two. It may be that these particular measurements — a dozen acoustic summaries and a genre tag — are too coarse, and that richer evidence would do better: the audio itself rather than summaries of it, the artist's existing audience, the release date, the promotional spend, the playlist placements. Or it may be that most of what decides a song's fate was never in the recording to begin with. The evidence here cannot separate those two, but it does lean. Six methods of increasing power, all given the same evidence, converged on the same ceiling rather than climbing steadily toward one; that pattern is more typical of evidence that has been exhausted than of methods that are not yet good enough. A sensible next step would not be a seventh model. It would be different evidence.
