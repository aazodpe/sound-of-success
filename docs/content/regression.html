Regression

Every method so far has sorted songs into boxes. Regression asks a different question: not which group a song belongs to, but how much. This tab fits a line through popularity itself, and then uses the same machinery to put a probability on charting — which finally reveals something the classifiers had been hiding.

Overview

Linear regression predicts a number. It assumes the thing being predicted can be written as a weighted sum of the inputs plus a constant, and it chooses those weights to make the total squared error as small as possible. The output is unbounded and continuous, which makes it the right tool for a quantity like popularity, measured here from 0 to 100. Each weight has a plain reading: it is how much the prediction moves when that one input rises by one unit and everything else is held still. That interpretability is the method's real attraction, because unlike a tree or a boosted ensemble it hands back a sentence you can argue with.

Logistic regression predicts a class, despite the name. It begins the same way, by computing a weighted sum of the inputs, but that sum — the log-odds, or score — runs from minus infinity to plus infinity and so cannot be a probability. The sigmoid function fixes this. It takes any number and squashes it into the range 0 to 1: a very negative score maps close to 0, a very positive score close to 1, and a score of exactly 0 maps to 0.5. The curve is steepest in the middle, meaning the model is most sensitive to changes in evidence when it is genuinely undecided and increasingly indifferent once it is already confident.

Turning that probability into a class takes one more step, and it is a choice rather than a consequence. A threshold is applied: above it the answer is yes, below it no. The default is 0.5, which is only the right answer when the two classes are equally common and the two kinds of mistake cost the same. Neither holds here — 13.2% of songs charted, so a model can be right 86.8% of the time by never saying yes at all. The threshold therefore does real work in this project, and the Results section shows exactly how much.

Both methods are being used here to attack the project's central question from a new angle. Classification has so far asked whether a song charts, and has answered by refusing to commit. Linear regression sidesteps that by predicting a continuous popularity score, where there is no threshold to hide behind and the error can be stated in the same units as the thing being predicted. Logistic regression then returns to charting, but reports probabilities rather than verdicts — and probabilities can be checked for whether they rank songs correctly even when the verdicts are useless.

Data Prep

Both models are supervised, so both need labelled data and a split that keeps the scoring honest. This module also adds a third partition. Because the assignment requires that settings be chosen without consulting the final test set, the 25,817 labelled songs were divided into 15,489 for training, 5,164 for validation, and 5,164 for testing. All three are verified disjoint. Tuning decisions are made against validation; the test set is read once, at the end.

The split reproduces the one used in Module 3 with the same seed and the same stratification, and the script asserts that the held-out songs match row for row. That matters because the comparison this tab draws against the earlier Naïve Bayes model is only meaningful if both were scored on identical songs, which they were.

The two models need different targets, and the distinction is the heart of the data preparation here:

ModelTarget columnWhy that columnFeatures
Linear regression track_popularity Linear regression predicts a number, so it needs a continuous target. Popularity runs 0 to 100 with a mean of 40.6 and a standard deviation of 23.2. The binary charted flag cannot be used here. The 10 numeric audio measurements
Logistic regression charted Logistic regression predicts a probability between two outcomes, so it needs a two-class label. 1 if the song reached the Billboard Hot 100, 0 otherwise — the same target Module 3 used. The same 10, standardised

For logistic regression the features were standardised to mean zero and unit variance, fitted on the training rows only. This is not required by the method but it makes the coefficients comparable with one another: without it, a coefficient on tempo (measured in the hundreds) and one on danceability (measured between 0 and 1) could not be read side by side. Linear regression was fitted on the raw values so that its coefficients keep their natural units, with a standardised version computed alongside purely for comparing magnitudes.

Results

Linear regression: predicting popularity

On the held-out songs the model achieves an RMSE of 22.49 and an MAE of 18.67 points, with an R² of 0.062. Those numbers need a reference point to mean anything. Predicting the training average for every single song — a model that ignores the music entirely — gives an RMSE of 23.22. So ten measurements of what a song sounds like improve the typical error by 0.73 points out of 100. The R² says the same thing in different units: 6.2% of the variation in popularity is accounted for, and 93.8% is not.

Two coefficients in plain words. The coefficient on loudness is +1.16, which in natural units means that making a track one decibel louder is associated with roughly one extra point of popularity, holding the other nine measurements fixed. The coefficient on instrumentalness is −10.24, meaning a track at the fully instrumental end of the scale is predicted about ten points less popular than an otherwise identical track with vocals — by some distance the most interpretable effect in the model, and consistent with what every earlier module found about vocals.

Those readings come with a caveat worth stating rather than burying. "Holding the others fixed" is doing heavy lifting in a dataset where energy and loudness move together. Because the model can trade one against the other, their individual coefficients are unstable even though their combined effect is not, which is precisely why the standardised chart shows two large opposing bars rather than one moderate one. The honest summary is that loudness and energy jointly matter a little, and that attributing the effect to either one separately is not supported.

Logistic regression: predicting charting

One coefficient in terms of the odds. Logistic coefficients are additive on the log-odds scale, so exponentiating one turns it into a multiplier on the odds themselves. For instrumentalness the odds ratio is 0.396: a one-standard-deviation increase in how instrumental a track is multiplies its odds of charting by about 0.40, which is to say it cuts those odds by roughly 60%. Vocals are the single strongest thing this model has to say about charting, and it says it clearly.

But logistic regression also reports something Naïve Bayes did not, and it changes the picture. Its ROC AUC is 0.681. AUC measures how often the model gives a genuine hit a higher probability than a non-hit, where 0.5 is a coin flip and 1.0 is perfect. At 0.681 the model is ranking songs meaningfully better than chance — it is not blind. It simply never becomes confident enough to cross a 0.5 threshold, because when only 13 songs in 100 chart, even a decent ranking rarely pushes any individual song past even odds.

Conclusions

Linear regression gave the clearest answer yet to the question this project has been circling, because it reports its failure in units anyone can understand. Knowing ten things about how a record sounds improves a guess at its popularity by three quarters of a point on a hundred-point scale. Not three quarters of a standard deviation — three quarters of a point. Put the other way, 93.8% of what makes one song more popular than another is invisible to these measurements. After three modules of weak correlations and baseline-matching classifiers, this is the same conclusion stated in the plainest possible terms.

The coefficients are nonetheless interpretable, and two of them say something real. Vocals matter: a fully instrumental track is predicted about ten points less popular, and on the charting side being one standard deviation more instrumental cuts the odds of charting by around 60%. That is the single most consistent finding in the entire project, visible in Module 1's raw comparison of charted against non-charted songs, in the decision tree's splits, and now in two regressions fitted independently. Popular music, almost definitionally, has someone singing on it.

The more interesting result is one earlier modules could not have produced. Logistic regression matched Naïve Bayes exactly on accuracy and recall — both effectively refuse to predict a hit — but its ROC AUC of 0.681 shows the refusal is not blindness. The model can rank songs by their chances better than chance would. What it cannot do is convert that ranking into a confident yes. This distinction matters, because "the model learned nothing" and "the model learned something too weak to act on" are different claims, and only the second one is true. Three modules of accuracy figures had been quietly hiding that difference.

Re-weighting the classes makes the trade-off explicit and settles the practical question. Force the model to find hits and it finds 65% of them — while being wrong about four out of every five songs it flags. For anyone deciding where to put a marketing budget that is not a usable tool, and no change of threshold fixes it, because the underlying ranking is only modestly better than chance. What AdaBoost offers on the next tab is a chance to improve that ranking rather than merely re-slice it, which is now the only avenue left worth testing.
